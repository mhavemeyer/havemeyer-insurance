CSA in plain English: the seven BASICs and what they measure

What FMCSA's Compliance, Safety, Accountability program actually tracks, where the data comes from, and why it matters beyond enforcement.

If you run trucks, you've heard of CSA. It's one of the most talked-about and least understood parts of trucking safety. Here's the short version of what it is and how it works.

What CSA is

CSA stands for Compliance, Safety, Accountability. It's the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration's (FMCSA) program for identifying motor carriers that may have safety problems and prioritizing them for intervention. The part most owners deal with is the Safety Measurement System (SMS), which organizes a carrier's safety data into categories.

Where the data comes from

SMS draws on information FMCSA and state partners collect, mainly roadside inspection results (including violations and out-of-service orders), state-reported crash reports, and findings from investigations. Every inspection, clean or not, becomes part of your record.

The seven BASICs

The data is grouped into seven Behavior Analysis and Safety Improvement Categories, or BASICs:

  1. Unsafe Driving: dangerous or careless operation, such as speeding, improper lane changes, texting or not wearing a seat belt.
  2. Crash Indicator: a carrier's history of crash involvement, based on state-reported crashes.
  3. Hours-of-Service (HOS) Compliance: driving over hours limits, and logbook or ELD problems.
  4. Vehicle Maintenance: brakes, lights, tires and other mechanical defects, plus failing to make required repairs.
  5. Controlled Substances/Alcohol: use or possession of drugs or alcohol, and related testing-program issues.
  6. Hazardous Materials (HM) Compliance: unsafe handling of hazmat, such as leaking containers or missing placards and shipping papers.
  7. Driver Fitness: drivers who are unqualified, for example lacking a valid CDL or medical certificate.

How the measurement works, in general terms

SMS doesn't just count violations. It weights them by how severe they are and how recent they are, and it compares carriers against others with similar amounts of activity. Results are expressed as percentiles, and carriers above an intervention threshold in a BASIC can be prioritized for warning letters, investigations or other follow-up. FMCSA has proposed changes to how SMS is calculated, so check FMCSA's own resources for the current methodology rather than relying on older rules of thumb.

What's public and what isn't

Since federal legislation in 2015 (the FAST Act), percentiles and alerts for most property carriers haven't been shown on FMCSA's public SMS website. Inspection, violation and crash details are still widely available, though, and plenty of people in the industry, including shippers, brokers and insurance underwriters, look at that underlying data.

Why this matters beyond enforcement

It's easy to think of CSA only as a DOT issue. In practice, your safety record is one of the clearest signals outsiders have about how your operation runs. That's why I think every owner should look at their own data at least as often as an underwriter or a customer does.

A practical first step

Log in to FMCSA's SMS with your USDOT number and PIN, and look at which BASICs have the most activity and which specific violations keep showing up. Repeat violations usually point to a fixable process, like pre-trip inspections, maintenance follow-up or log review.

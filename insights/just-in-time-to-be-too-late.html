“Just in Time to Be Too Late”: When the Driver Is on Time and the Dock Isn't

By Michael Havemeyer, TRS, TCS · Nov. 10, 2026

If your driver arrived on time, can the trucking company or the broker still get hit financially? Yes, though not always in the way people expect.

To quote Hank Williams, "You are just in time to be too late."

Imagine a cable company gives you a 9:00 AM to noon arrival window. The technician shows up at 2:00 PM, then hands you a bill for being late. Sounds ridiculous, right? Yet that's how many businesses feel when they're penalized for delays they didn't create.

The call usually starts the same way. The driver checked in on time. Then the truck sat: no door, a shift change, a full yard. Three hours later the trailer is empty, the driver's hours are nearly gone, and the next receiver is asking where the truck is. Fleet owners ask a simple question: if we arrived on time, can the trucking company or the broker still get hit financially?

The short answer is yes, not always in the way people expect.

On the stop where your driver actually made the appointment, the major retail programs I reviewed generally measure "on time" at the gate, not when the last pallet comes off the trailer. Kroger's official 2025 vendor-compliance update defines on-time delivery as the purchase order being gated in at the distribution center on the Original Requested Arrival Date. The 3 percent ORAD compliance fee applies only to late purchase orders caused by the vendor. A separate $200 fee applies when a shipment is more than 30 minutes late to the scheduled appointment at gate-in. Many of those fees also do not apply when Kroger manages the inbound lane. A slow unload after a timely gate-in should not, by itself, trigger those late-arrival charges.

Walmart's on-time measure is described the same way by trade sources that work inside supplier portals. If a delivery appointment was scheduled, the Arrival Gate Timestamp is treated as the Arrival Compliance Date. If no appointment was booked, Walmart may use the "Pro Date," the day it processes the shipping paperwork, which can be days after the truck arrived. That is a documentation problem, not an unloading problem. Those Walmart details come from consultant reporting, not a public Walmart page, and should be confirmed in Retail Link.

So why do fleets still lose money when the dock is the delay?

First, detention. The truck is still sitting, and the hours-of-service clock is still running. ATRI's September 2024 study found that 52 percent of truckload drivers ran out of available on-duty time at a customer facility because of detention at least once in 2023. Waiting is not free, even when it is not the carrier's fault.

Second, the cascade. Detention at Stop 1 makes the driver late to Stop 2. ATRI reported that 57.8 percent of truckload drivers were late to, or had to cancel, a later pickup or delivery at least once in 2023 because they were detained at the previous facility. The fine, if there is one, often attaches to the second appointment, not the facility that caused the wait.

Third, collection is weak. ATRI found that 94.5 percent of fleets charge detention fees, but carriers invoice only 75 percent of incidents, and only 55 percent of those invoices are paid. For truckload fleets, fees were collected on just 43.5 percent of all detention incidents. The median truckload fee was $50 an hour, against $99.54 in non-fuel revenue per hour. A carrier may also be unable to bill detention if the driver was late to the appointment, even if the wait that followed was much longer.

Fourth, some broker and shipper contracts deduct a late fee from the settlement whether or not the carrier caused the delay. Owner-operators have described $250 late-arrival deductions in rate confirmations. Those are examples, not an industry average, but the contract language is what matters.

In 2018, the U.S. Department of Transportation's Office of Inspector General estimated that a 15-minute increase in average dwell time raises the expected crash rate by 6.2 percent. It also associated detention with $1.1 billion to $1.3 billion in reduced driver earnings and $250.6 million to $302.9 million in reduced carrier net income in the for-hire truckload sector. I did not find a 2024 through 2026 federal law that sets detention-time standards. A truck-parking grant bill has been introduced, and the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration is collecting new detention data and has proposed a limited pilot that would let participating drivers pause the 14-hour window, including for detention. Until then, this remains a contract and documentation problem.

Don't assume insurance will close the gap

Standard auto and motor truck cargo policies generally will not pay a late fee, a missed-appointment deduction, or a retailer's OTIF chargeback. Cargo forms commonly exclude delay, loss of use, and loss of market, and some also exclude contractual fines. Auto liability is built for injury and property damage from an accident, not a service-failure penalty in a rate confirmation. If a customer contract passes those charges through to the carrier, that exposure is usually uninsured.

What I tell fleets

What I tell the fleets I work with is practical. Keep the record that proves you were on time: the appointment confirmation, the ELD and geofence arrival timestamp, the gate-in or guard-shack time, and a note if the dock was not ready. If a receiver later scores the load late, or a broker deducts a fee, that file is the dispute. Review detention clauses before you haul the freight: when the clock starts, what "carrier-controllable" means, and whether a chargeback can be passed through regardless of cause. Do not assume a timely arrival will be obvious later, and do not assume insurance will close the gap. It usually will not.

If your drivers are arriving on time and still leaving a facility behind on hours, money, or the next appointment, it is worth a closer look at the contracts and the paper trail.

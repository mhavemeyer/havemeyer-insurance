Carriers, Capacity, and the Next Level of Trucking Logistics

By Michael Havemeyer, TRS, TCS · Nov. 7, 2026

Aligned incentives, regulatory friction, and the case that operating carriers, not large logistics intermediaries, will drive the next wave of innovation.

What if the future of trucking won't be built by the biggest logistics companies? Over the past year, a series of regulatory, operational, and technology-related developments have quietly reduced available trucking capacity. While many focus on the growing influence of large brokers, 3PLs, and 4PLs, there's another possibility worth exploring: the next wave of innovation may come from the carriers themselves.

Introduction

Craig Fuller, founder and CEO of FreightWaves, recently argued that the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration's enforcement crackdown is effectively permanent. Small carriers and brokers hoping for a future loosening of rules should not expect relief, he said, because the industry's largest fleets now want tighter standards. "Follow the money," Fuller stated at Optym's Convergence 2026 conference, as reported by FreightWaves. The largest motor carriers, along with the American Trucking Associations and the Truckload Carriers Association, view stricter driver-hiring standards as "desperately needed" and "the only way that they will survive."

Reporting over the past year shows a combination of deliberate industry support for tougher rules, large carriers and brokers actively shrinking the pools of carriers they will use, and technical problems with FMCSA's new Motus registration system that have grounded thousands of mostly small operators. The result has been a measurable reduction in available capacity, even as freight demand has remained relatively soft.

This pattern is best understood not as a conspiracy but as a Nash equilibrium of aligned incentives. At the same time, a distinct thesis deserves attention: the operating carriers themselves, not the large third-party logistics (3PL) and fourth-party logistics (4PL) organizations, are the ones positioned to drive genuine innovation that takes trucking and logistics to the next level.

Motus and the suspension wave

Motus, FMCSA's new registration system, went live for all carriers in mid-May 2026, replacing key functions of the older Portal and Unified Registration System. It was designed to strengthen identity verification, confirm principal places of business, and help identify "chameleon" carriers that shut down and reopen under new names to evade enforcement. At the same time, the rollout has produced widespread technical problems.

In late October 2026, analysis by the Central Analysis Bureau and Commercial Carrier Journal found that more than 6,475 companies had their bodily-injury and property-damage insurance requirements changed, often from $750,000 or below to $1 million or higher, triggering involuntary suspensions. Roughly 6,000 of those changes occurred in a short period around October 27. One documented case involved a long-standing Illinois flatbed and step-deck carrier that was erroneously reclassified as a hazmat operator. Its authority was suspended, it lost the ability to book loads, and it received no advance notice.

FMCSA Chief Safety Officer Steve Dowling has acknowledged that roughly 5 percent of registration activity has faced problems while emphasizing that Motus is performing its intended screening function. For many small fleets, even a temporary inactive status is enough to lose freight, because brokers and shippers routinely check authority before tendering loads.

Large carriers and brokers shrink their approved lists

At the same time, major carriers and brokers have been deliberately reducing the number of carriers they will work with. Trade press has reported that Schneider National cut its brokerage approved-carrier network from 60,000 to 14,000, a reduction of roughly 77 percent, and that Knight-Swift reduced its approved brokerage carrier pool by about 70 percent over roughly two years, citing cargo theft, highway safety, and broker liability. C.H. Robinson has also been trimming its carrier base following the Supreme Court's Montgomery decision and a large jury verdict in a negligent-hiring case.

These reductions are not marginal. They remove tens of thousands of carriers from the pools that actually receive freight from large shippers and brokers, regardless of whether those carriers remain active in FMCSA systems.

Aligned incentives and Nash equilibrium

The record does not show a single coordinated campaign, but it does show aligned incentives. Large asset carriers and the trade associations that represent them have concluded that the surge of low-cost, non-compliant capacity that entered during and after the pandemic boom undercut their economics. Liability pressure after Montgomery gives large brokers clear reasons to shrink their approved lists. Motus adds a technical overlay.

This is a classic Nash equilibrium outcome. Each major player is optimizing for its own interests given what the others are doing. No single large player has a strong incentive to reverse course unilaterally. Smaller carriers cannot unilaterally force their way back onto the major lists. Their best individual options are compliance, niching, or exiting, none of which reverses the broader equilibrium. You do not need a coordinated plot to explain why small carriers are being squeezed from multiple directions at once.

The carrier-led innovation thesis

A personal perspective that deserves serious consideration is that the operating carriers, the companies that actually run trucks, manage drivers, and move freight, are the ones who will bring about the innovation that takes trucking and logistics to the next level. Larger organizations, particularly large 3PLs and 4PLs, are by nature slow to move and adopt.

Carriers sit closest to the operational realities: equipment utilization, driver retention, route efficiency, maintenance costs, customer service at the dock, and the daily friction of compliance. Innovations that matter, whether in telematics integration, dynamic routing that accounts for real detention patterns, predictive maintenance that reduces roadside failures, or new models for driver pay and scheduling, tend to emerge from operators who feel those problems directly.

The current equilibrium of tighter screening and capacity discipline may actually create space for this carrier-led innovation. As the pool of marginal or non-compliant capacity is reduced, the remaining carriers that can operate reliably have more opportunity to differentiate on service, technology, and efficiency rather than pure price. Those who invest in real operational improvements, not just compliance theater, stand to capture share.

Implications

The Nash equilibrium of aligned incentives explains the durability of the squeeze without requiring conspiracy. At the same time, the carrier-led innovation thesis suggests that the same environment may reward operators who solve real problems rather than those who primarily intermediate them. The innovators will be the carriers.
